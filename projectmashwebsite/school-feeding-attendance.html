How School Feeding Programs Boost Attendance in Mathare — Hunger vs. Learning

December 2025 Mathare, Nairobi

You've probably heard it before — "a hungry child can't learn." Globally, an estimated 66 million primary-aged children go to school hungry every day. We've seen exactly what that looks like in Mathare, and exactly what changes when it stops.

School feeding is one of the largest social safety nets on earth — the World Food Programme estimates around $48 billion is spent on it annually, reaching roughly 418 million children worldwide. The reason governments and NGOs invest at that scale isn't sentiment; it's evidence. Hunger measurably reduces a child's concentration, memory, and willingness to stay in the classroom, and when household budgets are tight, an unreliable school meal is often the deciding factor in whether a child shows up at all.

Kenya has decades of experience with this exact trade-off. From the "Nyayo Milk" program of the 1970s to today's Home-Grown School Meals Programme, the pattern keeps repeating in the research: when the lunch levy becomes unaffordable, attendance turns irregular; when children can't concentrate from hunger, learning suffers; when a feeding program lapses, dropout risk climbs. Rising food costs have pushed the price of a single school meal in Kenya from roughly $1.23 to $2.50 per student per day in just a few years — squeezing exactly the families who can least absorb it.

What We See in Mathare

That's why every Roots & Rise learning session at YouthRise Changemakers begins with a warm cup of uji (porridge) for every child — not as a bonus, but as a core, non-negotiable part of the program. We've watched the same pattern the global data predicts play out in our own sessions: children who arrive hungry struggle to stay focused through a lesson; children who've eaten, stay, engage, and come back the next day.

"A nutritious meal isn't charity on top of education — it's the floor that makes education possible at all."

A cup of porridge costs little. What it buys — a child who can actually concentrate long enough to learn to read — is, by every measure the World Bank and WFP track, one of the highest-return investments in development. We intend to keep that cup full for as long as Mathare's children need it.

Sources: World Food Programme, School Meals (wfp.org); World Bank, How Kenya's School Meals Program is Feeding Minds and Building Resilience (2024); systematic review, Perceived Benefits and Challenges of School Feeding Programs (PMC, 2024).
